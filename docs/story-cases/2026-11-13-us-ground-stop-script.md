## Narration (223 words)

America stopped nearly every departing flight. But about 3,500 planes were already in the sky. What happened to them?

On Friday, November 13, at 3:36 p.m., U.S. air traffic controllers began seeing unstable radar and communications information. The FAA ordered a nationwide ground stop.

Controllers and pilots brought the aircraft already flying down safely. At 8:15 that evening, the stop was lifted.

You might think that was the end.

It wasn't.

By Saturday evening, airlines had canceled more than 6,150 U.S. flights that day and delayed thousands more. Reopening the airspace couldn't instantly put planes and crews back where they needed to be.

Then came a preliminary explanation.

Officials said that during work replacing aging copper communications lines with fiber, a router generated more than a thousand connection attempts every few seconds, overwhelming parts of the network.

They don't believe it was a cyberattack. But investigators haven't settled whether faulty equipment or installation work was responsible. The contractor involved, L3Harris, says it's helping the investigation.

The FAA expects flight schedules to largely recover by late Sunday. Yet hundreds of Sunday flights were already canceled.

So here's the question: Will Sunday finish with fewer than one thousand U.S. flight cancellations, or one thousand or more?

Make your prediction. The published numbers will tell us who was right.

The ground stop ended. The investigation didn't.
